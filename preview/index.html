02 / Long-form chapter · Respiratory system

The first breath is a change of worlds.

A fetus has lungs, a diaphragm, airways and pulmonary vessels. Yet its lungs are not responsible for exchanging oxygen and carbon dioxide. Then, around birth, the entire arrangement changes: an organ built in liquid must become an air-filled exchange surface, while the circulation redirects blood toward it. The intriguing question is not simply when breathing begins. It is how the lungs become capable of breathing.

How the mature system actually works

Follow a molecule of oxygen. A contraction of the diaphragm enlarges the thoracic cavity, lowering alveolar pressure below atmospheric pressure so air enters. The conducting airways distribute that air, but most gas exchange takes place across a very thin alveolar–capillary barrier. Oxygen moves down its partial-pressure gradient into pulmonary capillary blood and binds to haemoglobin. Carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction, with much of its blood transport occurring as bicarbonate.

Ventilation alone is not enough. The alveolus must receive blood as well as air. Regional differences in ventilation and perfusion, together with diffusion properties and haemoglobin concentration, influence oxygen delivery. Breathing is regulated by brainstem networks responding to chemical and neural information; the respiratory muscles must generate sufficient pressure against airway and tissue resistance.

Go deeper · pressure, resistance and the work of breathing

For quiet inspiration, diaphragm contraction increases thoracic volume and makes intrapleural pressure more negative. Transpulmonary pressure (alveolar minus pleural pressure) helps distend the lung. Lung compliance describes volume change per unit pressure change; elastic recoil and surface tension oppose expansion. For a simplified rigid tube with laminar flow, resistance follows the Hagen–Poiseuille relation and is inversely proportional to the fourth power of radius. Real airways branch, deform and experience different flow regimes, so this is a conceptual approximation rather than a bedside prediction.

At the alveolar interface, Fick’s law predicts that diffusion increases with surface area and partial-pressure difference, and decreases with membrane thickness. Haemoglobin binding maintains oxygen uptake, while pulmonary capillary transit and ventilation–perfusion matching shape effective exchange.

Why surfactant matters

The inner surface of an alveolus is lined by a thin liquid film. Surface tension at an air–liquid interface tends to resist expansion. Type II alveolar epithelial cells secrete surfactant, a mixture rich in phospholipids with specialized proteins, that lowers this tension. Surfactant therefore reduces the pressure needed to inflate and stabilize gas-exchange units and helps prevent their collapse at low lung volumes.

Go deeper · Laplace’s law and its limits

For an ideal spherical interface, the pressure difference is approximately ΔP = 2T/r, where T is surface tension and r is radius. This suggests why small airspaces are especially sensitive to surface tension. Real alveoli are not independent perfect spheres: their geometry, interdependence, surfactant redistribution and recruitment dynamics matter. The equation is a useful starting model, not a literal description of every alveolus.

Birth is a cardiopulmonary event

Before delivery, pulmonary vascular resistance is relatively high, and much of the right ventricular output bypasses the pulmonary circulation through fetal shunts. Lung aeration and rising oxygen tension help relax the pulmonary vascular bed, increasing blood flow through the lungs. Simultaneously, removal of the low-resistance placenta changes systemic circulation. Increasing pulmonary venous return and altered atrial pressures favour functional closure of the foramen ovale; the ductus arteriosus changes flow and subsequently constricts.

Birth-related fluid clearance is more complicated than the idea that the birth canal simply squeezes water out. Pressure-driven movement of liquid from distal airspaces, epithelial ion transport and subsequent interstitial clearance all contribute. The first hours are therefore a process of establishing and maintaining aeration, not a single instantaneous event.

Go deeper · epithelial sodium transport and pulmonary vascular signals

Fetal pulmonary epithelium secretes liquid during development. Near birth, hormonal changes and epithelial ion-transport processes favour sodium absorption, with water following osmotic gradients. Breathing-generated pressure also redistributes liquid across the distal lung epithelium into interstitial compartments. In pulmonary arteries, improved oxygenation and endothelial nitric oxide signalling contribute to smooth-muscle relaxation and reduced vascular resistance. These mechanisms operate together, not as independent on/off switches.

How development continues

The newborn lung is not a finished adult lung in miniature. Airway calibre, chest-wall mechanics, distal gas-exchange architecture, pulmonary vessels, respiratory muscles and neural control all change at different rates. Postnatal alveolarization and vascular remodelling continue through childhood; the precise timing and extent of continued alveolar formation are subjects of ongoing study. Growth through adolescence increases pulmonary volumes and exercise capacity, while the mature system remains susceptible to disease, environmental exposures and ageing.

Interpretation, not diagnosis. This chapter explains normal physiology. Persistent fast breathing, grunting, chest retractions, bluish colour or difficulty feeding in a newborn warrant urgent clinical assessment; an interactive guide cannot assess an individual baby.
Reading map

Mechanism: pressure → airflow → diffusion → blood transport → neural feedback.

Development: branching → surfactant and vascular maturation → aeration → postnatal growth → mature regulation.

Cross-system connection: the heart and lungs reorganize together at birth.

Evidence caution: precise ages of alveolar completion and the contribution of individual fluid-clearance mechanisms should not be overspecified.

Known limitations before publication

Edition-specific chapters in commercial textbooks remain to be validated. Longitudinal claims need a dedicated age-stratified literature review, especially for postnatal alveolarization and respiratory neural control.
